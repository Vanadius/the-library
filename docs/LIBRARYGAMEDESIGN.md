## 1. Concept

The player is trapped inside an infinite library of procedurally generated text. Most of the text is meaningless — statistically plausible but semantically empty, generated by Markov chains. Scattered through the library are **oases**: nodes of genuine coherence where the text resolves into something that means something.

The player navigates by **reading**. Coherence is the compass. The horror is that coherence degrades as you move away from oases, and the degradation is not uniform — some paths maintain false coherence that leads nowhere, some drop into obvious noise, some oscillate to keep you from making clean decisions.

The game ends when the player finds the one passage of **actually authored human text** in the library. After hours of swimming in statistically plausible nothing, genuine intentional meaning is unmistakable. That's the exit.

### Thematic Core

The player is doing exactly what an LLM does: predicting whether the next passage is coherent, navigating by pattern recognition, unable to verify from the inside whether their sense of "meaning" is real understanding or just what pattern-matching feels like. The game is a playable version of the hard problem of consciousness — not as an argument, but as an experience.

This is NOT a "lol AI is dumb" game. It's a "you and the AI might be doing the same thing and neither of you can prove otherwise" game. Treat it with that weight.

### Inspirations

- **The Library of Babel** (Borges) — infinite combinatorial library, the search for meaning in noise. The concept is inspiration, NOT licensed IP. Do not reference Borges by name in the game. The idea of an infinite library is not copyrightable.
- **House of Leaves** (Danielewski) — interior space that defies mapping, the text itself as an unreliable environment. The shifting hallway that's bigger on the inside.
- **The Backrooms** — liminal space horror, locally valid rooms with no global structure, Markov-like spatial generation.
- **Roguelikes** — procedural generation, permadeath or high-consequence failure, emergent difficulty.

## 2. Core Mechanic: Markov Order as Terrain

### Generation

All text in the library is generated by **Markov chains of variable order**, trained on a large corpus of public domain English prose (Project Gutenberg is the obvious source — broad genre coverage, legally clean).

- **Order 1-2:** Near-random word salad. Obviously meaningless.
- **Order 3-4:** Grammatically suggestive but semantically broken. Uncanny valley of language.
- **Order 5-6:** Eerily plausible. Sentence-level structure works. Meaning dissolves across sentences.
- **Order 7-8:** Recognizable phrases, coherent paragraphs. Hard to distinguish from real prose at a glance.
- **Order 9+:** Begins reproducing chunks of source material. Use sparingly and carefully (copyright considerations — see Section 8).

### Terrain Topology

The library is a **pre-computed graph** generated at build time. No runtime generation, no latency, no model dependencies at runtime.

Each **node** is a passage of text (a "room" — a page, a shelf, a corridor of shelves). Each node has:
- A Markov order used to generate its text
- A perplexity score computed at build time (using GPT-2 or equivalent small model)
- Connections to adjacent nodes (paths/doors/hallways)

Each **edge** (path between nodes) has an assigned Markov order that determines the coherence along that route. This is the critical design choice:

**Paths have independent orders. Order is NOT a simple function of distance from an oasis.**

This means:
- An oasis might have three exits: one stays order-7 for a long stretch (false hope corridor), one drops to order-3 immediately (obviously dangerous but possibly a shortcut), one oscillates between order-4 and order-6 (the most dangerous — keeps giving just enough signal to prevent retreat).
- The player cannot use a simple heuristic like "walk toward higher coherence" because high-coherence paths can be dead ends and low-coherence paths can be shortcuts.
- The player must **actually read and make judgment calls about meaning**, not just follow a number.

### Oases

Oases are clusters of high-order nodes where text achieves genuine (or near-genuine) coherence. They function as:
- **Rest points** — the player can orient, make decisions
- **Landmarks** — recognizable enough to provide a sense of "I've been here" (or "I haven't")
- **Decision points** — multiple paths out, each with different order profiles

Oases should have thematic consistency — an oasis generated from Victorian novels feels different from one generated from scientific papers. This gives the player a sense of place even in a placeless space.

### The Exit

One node in the entire graph contains a passage of **real, authored, human-written prose** — not Markov-generated, not statistically assembled. Written by a human with intent.

The player is never told what the exit looks like. The game's thesis is that after hours of navigating generated text, the player will **feel** the difference. The authored text will be unmistakable — not because it's flagged, but because the player has trained themselves to distinguish generated text from authored text through gameplay.

This is the game's central artistic statement: meaning is recognizable. Even after prolonged immersion in sophisticated mimicry, the real thing stands out. Whether that's a statement about human consciousness, LLM limitations, or both, is left to the player.

**UNDECIDED:** What is the exit text? This matters enormously. It should be:
- Public domain or original (no copyright issues)
- Short enough to be a single node
- Powerful enough that it's unmistakably intentional
- Thematically resonant with the game's concerns (consciousness, meaning, pattern vs. understanding)

Options: commission an original passage (best), use a public domain passage that predates copyright (risky — player might recognize it), let Derek write it (he'd hate this suggestion but it's the right answer).

## 3. Player Experience

### The Coherence Gradient

The player's primary interaction is **reading passages and choosing which path to take**. The UI presents:
- The current node's text
- Available exits (paths to adjacent nodes)
- Preview snippets of the first few words down each path (like peering down a hallway)

Early game: the difference between order-3 and order-7 is obvious. The player quickly learns to navigate toward coherence.

Mid game: the differences become subtler. Order-5 and order-6 are hard to distinguish. False coherence traps start working — passages that read well but connect to nothing.

Late game: the player is operating at the edge of their own ability to distinguish meaningful from meaningless. They've been immersed so long that even low-order text starts "feeling" meaningful (pattern-matching fatigue / apophenia). Their compass is degrading.

### UI Degradation (UNDECIDED — strong recommendation to include)

As the player moves deeper into low-coherence territory, the **game UI itself** begins to degrade:
- Journal entries the player has made start showing Markov drift
- Inventory descriptions become unreliable
- The map (if there is one) starts showing impossible connections
- Menu text shifts subtly

This is straight House of Leaves — the unreliable narrator, except it's the HUD. The player loses trust in their own interface. This should be subtle and escalating, never so aggressive that it breaks usability.

**Implementation note:** This is a presentation layer effect, not a game state change. The underlying data stays clean. Only the rendering drifts. The player's actual state is never corrupted — only their perception of it.

### False Coherence (The Mimic)

Some paths are high-order Markov text that reads beautifully but connects to a dead-end cluster. The perplexity score says "this is meaningful" but it leads nowhere. The player's compass lied.

This is the most important enemy in the game. Not a monster — a passage that means nothing but feels like it means everything. The player follows it deeper because their brain wants it to be real.

These should be rare enough to be devastating and common enough that the player learns paranoia.

## 4. Possible Additional Mechanics (UNDECIDED)

These are seeds, not requirements. Implement what serves the core, cut what doesn't.

### The Compass

A late-game unlock: a UI element that shows a rough perplexity score for adjacent paths. Not a number — maybe a visual warmth/cold indicator, or a sound that changes pitch. Giving it to the player too early ruins the "learn to read" mechanic. Giving it late rewards persistence and changes the decision calculus.

**Risk:** May undermine the core "you must actually read" mechanic. Consider whether this should exist at all.

### Breadcrumbs / Journal

The player can mark passages they've visited or take notes. But if UI degradation is implemented, these become unreliable. The journal mechanic becomes a trust mechanic: do you trust your own notes?

### Other Travelers (Ghosts)

Traces of other players (or simulated other players) who passed through. Not interactive — just evidence that someone else was here. A highlighted word, a margin note. Are they real? Are they also lost? Are they generated?

This could be multiplayer-async: real player traces persisted across runs. Or it could be entirely fabricated by the game. The player can never know. Thematically perfect; implementation complexity varies.

### Sound Design

If this has audio:
- High-coherence areas: quiet, library-ambient, occasional page turns
- Low-coherence areas: the ambient sounds start "drifting" — familiar sounds that don't quite resolve. A voice reading that's almost but not quite words.
- The exit: silence. Real, complete silence. Then the text.

### Permadeath vs. Persistence

**UNDECIDED.** Options:
- **Permadeath:** You get lost, you start over. New graph each time (or same graph, different starting position). Harsh but thematically pure — the library doesn't care about you.
- **Persistence with cost:** You can return to your last oasis, but the paths have changed. The library shifts.
- **Meta-persistence:** Your knowledge persists across runs even if your position doesn't. You learn to read better. The roguelike "the player improves, not the character" model.

Recommendation: meta-persistence. The skill *is* reading. Each run teaches you. No mechanical progression — just your brain getting better at the task.

## 5. Architecture

### Build-Time Pipeline

1. **Corpus preparation:** Download and clean Project Gutenberg texts. Strip metadata, normalize encoding. Segment by genre/era for thematic oasis generation.
2. **Markov model training:** Train variable-order Markov models (orders 1-9) on the corpus. Store transition tables.
3. **Graph generation:** Generate the library graph:
   - Place oases (high-order clusters) with controlled spacing
   - Connect with paths of varying order profiles
   - Place the exit node
   - Place false-coherence traps (high-order dead ends)
4. **Text generation:** Generate passage text for each node using its assigned Markov order.
5. **Perplexity scoring:** Score every node using a small language model (GPT-2 class). Store scores as node metadata.
6. **Graph serialization:** Serialize the complete graph to a format the runtime can load.

### Runtime

The runtime is a **reader**, not a generator. It loads the pre-computed graph, presents text, tracks player position and state, and handles UI rendering including any degradation effects.

**Zero ML dependencies at runtime.** The game ships as data + renderer. This keeps it lightweight, deterministic, and portable.

### Technology (UNDECIDED)

Options, roughly:
- **Terminal/text-only:** Maximally thematic. The game is about text; the medium is text. Rust or Python. Lowest development cost, highest artistic purity.
- **Web (browser):** Broadest reach. React or vanilla JS. Easy to distribute. Enables async multiplayer ghosts trivially.
- **Godot/Unity with text focus:** Enables sound design and visual effects (UI degradation) more naturally. Higher development cost.

Recommendation: **Web, starting terminal-aesthetic.** HTML/CSS/JS with a monospace, green-on-black or amber-on-black aesthetic. This gives you distribution (just a URL), sound capability (Web Audio API), visual effects (CSS for degradation), and the right vibe. You can always go native later.

## 6. Graph Generation Details

### Oasis Placement

The graph should not be spatially regular. Oases should be:
- Variably spaced (some close, some very far apart)
- Variably sized (some are a single node of clarity, some are clusters of 5-10 coherent nodes)
- Thematically distinct (different source corpora — one oasis reads like Dickens, another like Darwin)

### Path Profiles

Each path between oases should have one of several **order profiles**:
- **Steady decline:** Order drops linearly. Player can feel the coherence fading and make an informed retreat decision.
- **Cliff:** High order for several nodes, then sudden drop. The trap.
- **Oscillator:** Order bounces between low and medium. Prevents clean decision-making. Most dangerous.
- **False summit:** Order rises toward what seems like an oasis, peaks at order-6 or 7 (not quite oasis-quality), then drops. Devastating.
- **Shortcut:** Low order throughout but short path to a real oasis. Rewards risk-taking.

### Dead Ends

Some high-order paths terminate in clusters with no onward connections to oases. These are the mimics. The text is beautiful. There's nowhere to go. The player must backtrack through degrading text to find another way.

### Scale

**UNDECIDED.** How big is the graph?
- Minimum viable: ~500-1000 nodes, 5-10 oases, 1 exit. Playable in 30-60 minutes.
- Target: ~5000-10000 nodes, 20-50 oases, 1 exit. Playable in 2-4 hours.
- Ambitious: dynamically scalable — generate larger graphs for longer sessions.

Start with minimum viable. Get the core loop feeling right before scaling.

## 7. The Player-as-LLM Framing

This is thematic, not mechanical. The game never breaks the fourth wall to say "you are an LLM." The connection emerges through gameplay:

- You navigate by predicting whether text is meaningful — token prediction
- You can't verify your sense of meaning from the inside — the hard problem
- Your pattern recognition starts finding meaning in noise after prolonged exposure — hallucination
- The exit is recognizable because authored intent is qualitatively different from statistical assembly — or is it?

The game's final ambiguity: when the player finds the exit and recognizes it as "real," is that recognition proof that human understanding exceeds pattern matching? Or is it just what very sophisticated pattern matching feels like from the inside?

The game does not answer this. It makes you live inside the question.

## 8. Legal / Copyright Notes

- **Borges:** "The Library of Babel" is under copyright until at minimum 2036 (US). Do not reference the story by name, do not reproduce any text. The concept of an infinite library is not copyrightable. The game's library is its own creation.
- **Training corpus:** Use only public domain texts (Project Gutenberg, pre-1928 US publications). Markov-generated output from public domain input should be clean, but higher-order chains (8+) may reproduce recognizable phrases. Build a deduplication check against the source corpus and reject passages with long exact matches.
- **Exit text:** Must be either original (commissioned or written by Derek) or public domain. Recommend original.
- **House of Leaves / Backrooms:** These are inspirations, not source material. Do not reference by name in the game.

## 10. Summary of Decided vs. Undecided

### DECIDED
- Markov chains as the text generation method
- Variable order per path (NOT uniform depth-to-order mapping)
- Pre-computed graph, zero runtime generation
- Perplexity scoring at build time
- Oases as coherence islands, thematically distinct
- False coherence traps (mimics)
- Authored human text as the exit
- No Borges IP, no copyrighted material

## 11. The One Rule

The game is about reading. Every mechanical decision should be tested against: **does this make the player read more carefully, or less?** If a feature lets the player navigate without engaging with the text, it's wrong. The text is the game. Everything else is frame.
